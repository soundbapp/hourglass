# HOURGLASS — World Bible, Draft 1

*The Upside-Down Principle. Concept, era, characters, and image prompts.*

---

## 1. The Inversion

Three realms stacked vertically. That part is given. The idea that makes it a
world rather than a setting is what you find when you look at who is actually
*in* each one.

**Heaven is vast, rich, abundant — and nearly empty.**

**Hell is the hardest place to live — and the most populated, the most alive,
the only place where anything is made.**

**The Firmament exists to keep that arrangement in place.**

Heaven produces nothing. Every beautiful object in it was made in Hell and
carried up. The Firmament is the customs house, and what it confiscates on the
way through is not goods — it is *attribution*. You can send your work up. You
cannot go up with it.

That is the engine. It is satire with a real edge, it is true of every industry
anyone has ever worked in, and — crucially — it is **era-independent**. The
pitch room, the patent office, the chartered company, the A&R man, the
admissions committee, the algorithm: the Firmament has worn all of these faces
and the rule never changed. That is what lets the franchise walk from the
Bronze Age to the near future without the premise wearing out.

### The title

An hourglass has two chambers. Sand falls from the full one to the empty one.
Then someone turns it over.

**Heaven and Hell are the same place at different points in the turn.** The
vast abundant chamber empties; the crowded pit fills and becomes vast. The
Firmament is the neck — the only part that does not move, and the only part
that is narrow by design rather than by accident.

Nobody in the world knows this. The player finds out. That is the ending of
Game I and the premise of everything after.

## 2. The Three Realms

Each realm runs on one rule. Everything in it — architecture, light, economy,
how NPCs behave, what the camera does — should be derivable from that rule.

### ABOVE — Heaven

> **Rule: nothing here was made here.**

Vast, golden, abundant, and under-occupied. Terraces that run to the horizon.
Orchards nobody picks. Long tables set for a thousand with nine people at them.
Galleries of objects from every era, each on a pedestal, each with a small
**blank label**.

It is not sinister. It is *pleasant*. That is worse. The residents are kind,
unhurried, and completely incurious about where any of it came from. The
horror is the absence of the question.

The light is late-afternoon, low, warm, endless — a sun that never quite sets,
which means time doesn't pass, which means nothing is urgent, which means
nothing gets made.

**Visual key:** honey gold, bone white, pale linen. Deep focus, wide lenses,
enormous negative space. Figures tiny in frame.

### MIDDLE — The Firmament

> **Rule: the number of places is fixed.**

A narrow vertical world. Not a gate — a *court*, a *floor*, a *queue*, a
hearing that has been in session for four thousand years. Basalt and hard white
light. Tiers of assessors above you, faces in shadow, each with a tablet.

You stand on a disc of stone and you have the length of one petition to be
undeniable. There is no room at all for an impostor, because the assessors have
heard every pitch that has ever been made and they are *bored*, which is far
more dangerous than hostile. The energy is Shark Tank at the moment before
anyone speaks: everything hangs on whether you are interesting.

And because the number of places per cycle is fixed, **your success is somebody
else's rejection.** Not metaphorically. The ledger has their name in it.

The three species of the middle:

- **Assessors** — judge. Bored, fair-ish, unbribable, and almost impossible to
  surprise. Forty-two of them seated at any time.
- **Brokers** — represent you. Charming, genuinely useful, and they take a
  percentage. Of what, exactly, is left deliberately unclear until it isn't.
- **Scribes** — record. The least powerful and the most important, because the
  ledger outlives everyone and the ledger is the only thing Heaven ever reads.

**Visual key:** basalt black, cold white, one spot of red. Hard toplight,
volumetric haze, extreme verticality. Low angles. Long lenses that flatten the
tiers into a wall of faces.

### BELOW — Hell

> **Rule: everything is made here, by people who will never see it again.**

Not fire and demons. The hardest version of earth: a canyon city of forges and
looms and dye vats, stacked vertically, loud, crowded, brilliant, dangerous.
Survival of the fittest — but the fitness in question is *craft*. The best
smith eats. The best weaver's children eat.

Hell is where the culture is. The music, the food, the slang, the gods, the
jokes. Heaven has none of these; Heaven has the *objects*. This is the single
most important thing to get right in the art direction: **Hell must look like
the place you'd rather be.**

**Visual key:** oxide red, soot black, and then violent colour — indigo,
saffron, cochineal — because dye is one of the things they make. Hard noon sun,
dust, high contrast, telephoto compression to stack the levels.

## 3. Era: where Game I lands, and where the franchise walks

**Hourglass I: Heaven or Hell — Mesopotamia, c. 1750 BCE.**

The reasoning, because this is a real fork and you may want the other road:

- *Firmament* is not a fantasy word. It is **raqia**, the Babylonian-Hebrew
  cosmological dome — a literal solid vault separating the waters above from
  the waters below. The premise is already load-bearing in the source material.
- The ziggurat is architecture whose **only purpose is ascent**. Babel is the
  original story about an unauthorised attempt to go up, and the punishment is
  not death — it is being made *unintelligible*, which is exactly what the
  Firmament does to a petitioner it rejects.
- And the detail that sells the whole satire: **the earliest writing humans
  have is accounting.** Not poetry, not scripture — receipts. Who owed what to
  whom. The gatekeepers have been accountants from the first day there were
  gatekeepers. Clay tablets, cylinder seals, sealed envelopes of clay you must
  break to read. The Firmament's paperwork is the oldest technology in the
  world.

*The alternative:* **Egypt, the Hall of Two Truths** — forty-two assessor gods,
your heart weighed against a feather, a literal tribunal between worlds. It is
arguably an even cleaner fit for the Firmament specifically, and weaker for
Heaven and Hell. **Recommendation: Mesopotamia as the home, and steal the 42
assessors from Egypt.** Forty-two becomes the franchise's recurring number.

### The arc

Each release turns the glass. Same three realms, same rule, new face on the
Firmament.

| | Era | The Firmament is… | Heaven is… |
|---|---|---|---|
| **I** | Bronze Age, c. 1750 BCE | a court of scribes; clay, seals, ledgers | the terraces above the vault |
| **II** | Imperial Rome, c. 64 CE | patronage and the games; who sponsors you | the villa, the collection |
| **III** | Age of Sail, c. 1680 | the chartered company; manifests, letters of marque | the counting-house city |
| **IV** | Industrial, c. 1889 | the Exposition and the patent office; the exchange floor | the pavilion |
| **V** | Modern, c. 2008 | the pitch room, the boardroom, the crisis | the fund |
| **VI** | Near future | the model; verification, the API, the ranking | a server |

The joke that lands harder every single time: **it is the same room.** Same
proportions, same light from above, same disc on the floor, same forty-two
seats. Only the furniture changes. Players will notice this around Game III and
it will be a very good day for the franchise.

## 4. Characters

Rockstar protagonists are outsiders with one specific, undeniable competence,
climbing a system that is rigged in a way they half-know. Hold that.

**THE ASCENDANT** *(player)* — Born in Hell. Has one thing they are genuinely
the best at. Not chosen, not prophesied, not special — just *good*, in a world
where being good is necessary and nowhere near sufficient. The whole game is
the gap between those two words.

**THE ASSESSOR** — Seat 19 of 42. Has heard four thousand years of petitions.
Not cruel. Not corrupt. **Bored.** Will give you exactly the hearing the
statutes require and not one second more. The most frightening thing about them
is that they are being completely fair.

**THE BROKER** — Takes you on. Gets you in the room. Knows which assessor is
sitting, what they're tired of, what time of day to go. Charming, funny, warm,
and the single most useful person you will ever meet. Takes a percentage. The
reveal is not that he's a villain — it's what the percentage is *of*, and that
you agreed to it in writing, and that the writing is accurate.

**THE RETURNED** — Came back down. Nobody believes them, because why would
anyone leave Heaven? They left because it's empty, and they couldn't stand
being thanked for objects they didn't make. The only person in the world
telling the truth, and therefore read as mad.

**THE FORGER** — Manufactures credentials. In a place with *no room for
impostors* the impostor is the most dangerous person alive, and the most
sympathetic, because their argument is unanswerable: if the gate is arbitrary,
why is a forged key worse than a given one?

**THE WEIGHER** — Operates the scales. Possibly corrupt. Possibly the last
honest official in the Firmament. The game should never fully tell you, and
there should be solid evidence for both.

**THE GATEKEEPERS** — And here is the knife. *Every gatekeeper was once a
petitioner who failed.* They were not punished. They were **offered a job.**
Every bouncer was once in the line. Every assessor was once standing on the
disc. The system does not destroy the people it rejects — it *employs* them,
which is how it has lasted four thousand years.

## 5. Why this is natively multiplayer

The design falls out of the fiction instead of being bolted onto it.

**The quota is real.** The Firmament admits a fixed number per cycle. Server-wide,
persistent, visible. For you to go up, someone does not.

**The ledger is real.** The Firmament writes down everyone it rejects, and the
ledger is readable — by players, in the world, as an object. The names in it
are *other players*. You can look up someone you know. You can look up
yourself.

**Hell is co-operative, the Firmament is zero-sum, Heaven is almost solitary.**
Three realms, three completely different multiplayer textures, one world. The
loneliness of Heaven isn't a design compromise; it is the point, and the player
feels it as *gameplay* rather than being told it in a cutscene.

## 6. Easter eggs, hidden layers, and the Backrooms problem

The Backrooms works because it has **rules you discover and document**, and a
community that builds the lore outward faster than any studio could. Build for
that deliberately.

**The Statutes are published. The real rules are different.** The Firmament
prints its criteria. They are accurate and incomplete. The actual behaviour of
the assessors is observable, consistent, and *never stated anywhere* — which
means the player community will reverse-engineer it, write it down, and argue
about it. That is the cultural phenomenon. Don't document it. Make it true and
discoverable and leave it alone.

**Maker's marks.** Every object in Heaven has a mark hidden somewhere on it —
underside, inner seam, the third rivet. Every mark is a Hell name. Collect
them. Enough of them and you can prove the thing the entire cosmology depends
on nobody proving. This is the real ending.

**Blank labels.** Heaven's galleries label everything with empty plates. Nobody
has ever asked why. One of them is not blank.

**Forty-two.** Assessors, steps, seats, rungs on a ladder, days in a cycle. It
recurs across every era. Someone will count.

**The sand.** It is not sand. Look closely enough — there is a view distance at
which the ground resolves into something else, and the game never comments on
it.

**The turn.** Across the franchise, Heaven gets slightly emptier and Hell gets
slightly larger. Measurably. Comparably. Side-by-side-screenshot-able. By the
time anyone notices, it will have been happening for three games.

## 7. IMAGE PROMPTS — Google Flow

**How to use these.** One strong idea per prompt; generate four variations and
keep the one with the best *light*, not the best detail. The palette line is
the most load-bearing part of each prompt — it is what makes a set of images
look like one world. Era-anchored to Game I (Bronze Age) unless marked.

---

### ABOVE — HEAVEN

**H1 · The emptiness**
> A vast colonnaded terrace of pale gold stone stretching to a horizon of layered cloud, tier upon tier descending below, orchards heavy with unpicked fruit on every level. Long banquet tables set for hundreds. Only three distant figures in white linen, impossibly small at the far end. Low late-afternoon sun raking across the stone, long shadows, warm haze. Shot on 65mm, wide anamorphic, deep focus, natural light only. Palette: honey gold, bone white, pale sky. Overwhelming scale, almost nobody there.

**H2 · The gallery of blank labels**
> An immense vaulted gallery in warm ivory stone, filled floor to ceiling with objects from a hundred different civilisations — clay tablets, bronze figures, looms, instruments, carved doors — each on its own pedestal, each with a small blank brass label. Shafts of white light from high clerestory windows, dust drifting in the beams. A single curator in pale robes walking the aisle with a ledger. Cinematic wide, 35mm, natural light. Palette: ivory, honey, soft grey shadow.

**H3 · The sun that never sets**
> A wide hilltop of pale grass above the cloud layer, a single ancient olive tree, a stone bench, and a sun sitting permanently just above the horizon — golden hour that has lasted forever. Two figures seated, unhurried, facing away. Lens flare, warm rim light, long shadows pointing at the camera. Shot on 70mm, soft focus falloff. Palette: gold, white, dusty green. Beautiful and very slightly wrong.

**H4 · The waters above**
> Looking upward from a gold-paved Heaven terrace at a ceiling of water — an impossible suspended ocean overhead, light refracting through it in slow moving caustics, the silhouettes of enormous fish passing far above. Figures below barely glancing up. Wide-angle looking up, natural caustic light, high dynamic range. Palette: aquamarine, gold, white. Awe treated as ordinary.

**H5 · The arrival**
> A newly ascended figure in rough Bronze Age Hell clothing — dyed indigo wool, soot on the hands — standing at the edge of a vast gold terrace, still holding a tool. Clean, pale, elegantly dressed residents a hundred feet away, politely not looking. Wide shot, subject small and off-centre, hard warm sun. Palette: oxide red and indigo against honey gold and bone white. The colour contrast is the whole image.

**H6 · The storehouse**
> An enormous granary-vault of pale stone, grain heaped to the ceiling, spilling out of the doorway, more than anyone could eat in a hundred lifetimes. Nobody tending it. Birds in the high beams. Shaft of hard white light through a single high opening. Shot on 35mm, deep focus, dust-heavy air. Palette: wheat gold, bone, deep shadow.

---

### MIDDLE — THE FIRMAMENT

**F1 · The court** *(hero image — the one to get right)*
> A narrow vertical chamber of black basalt, no wider than a corridor but hundreds of feet tall, lit by a single hard slot of white light far above. One petitioner alone on a small stone disc at the centre of the floor, head tilted up. Ranked on tiers cut into both walls above them: dozens of seated assessors in grey, faces in shadow, each holding a clay tablet. Hard directional toplight, volumetric haze, deep black shadows. Anamorphic wide, low angle from behind the petitioner. Palette: basalt black, cold white, one spot of red.

**F2 · The ledger**
> Extreme close-up of a scribe's hands pressing a reed stylus into wet clay, cuneiform rows of names filling the tablet. Behind, out of focus, a wall of thousands of identical stacked tablets receding into darkness. Hard raking light from one side. Macro, shallow depth of field, 100mm. Palette: wet grey clay, cold white light, black.

**F3 · The queue**
> A compressed stone corridor packed with petitioners waiting, shoulder to shoulder, each clutching an object they made — a bolt of dyed cloth, a bronze blade, a sealed clay envelope. Faces lit from above by a narrow slot of light; everything below the shoulders in shadow. Telephoto, heavily compressed perspective, the line vanishing into darkness both ways. Palette: soot black, cold white, the dyed cloth the only colour in frame.

**F4 · The broker**
> A well-dressed man in fine pale wool leaning casually against black basalt, smiling, one hand on the shoulder of a nervous young craftsman in rough clothes. Behind them, the slot of white light and the tiers of seated assessors far above. Warm practical light on the broker's face, cold light on everything else. Medium shot, 50mm, shallow focus. Palette: cream and gold against basalt black and cold white. He is the most likeable person in the frame.

**F5 · The scales**
> A huge bronze balance suspended in a shaft of hard white light in a black stone hall. On one pan, a small clay tablet. On the other, nothing visible at all — and the empty pan is lower. An official in grey watching, expressionless. Low angle, strong volumetric beam, haze. Palette: black, cold white, oxidised bronze green.

**F6 · The ejection**
> Looking straight down a vast circular shaft from the underside of the Firmament — black stone walls dropping away, a tiny figure falling, already small, arms out, far below the glow of a red canyon city. Extreme wide, vertical, vertigo composition, top-down. Palette: black basalt walls, a distant furnace-red floor. No motion blur; perfectly still and silent.

---

### BELOW — HELL

**H-1 · The vertical city** *(hero image)*
> A canyon city carved into red rock under a white hazed sky, thousands of small workshops stacked vertically on both walls, smoke rising from a hundred forges, rope bridges and ladders spanning the gap, dyed cloth and laundry strung across the void. Crowded, loud, full of people. Hard noon sun, heavy dust, high contrast. Telephoto compressing the layers into one wall of life. Palette: oxide red, soot black, and violent indigo and saffron cloth.

**H-2 · The forge street**
> A narrow street at night lit entirely by open forge fires, Bronze Age smiths working bronze, sparks, steam, children carrying water, a dog, food cooking at a street stall. Warm orange firelight from below, deep blue night above. Handheld 35mm, shallow focus, grain. Palette: fire orange, deep indigo night, black. Warm, alive, the place you'd rather be.

**H-3 · The dye vats**
> Terraced open stone vats of dye on a red rock shelf — indigo, saffron, deep red — workers hanging enormous sheets of wet cloth on lines above them, colour running down the rock. Hard overhead sun, steam, stained hands and arms. High angle wide shot, 28mm. Palette: saturated indigo and saffron against oxide red stone. The most colourful image in the entire world bible.

**H-4 · Looking up**
> From the floor of the red canyon, looking straight up past a hundred levels of stacked workshops at a distant black ceiling — the underside of the Firmament — with one narrow slot of white light cutting down through the dust. Tiny figures on bridges silhouetted against it. Extreme vertical wide angle. Palette: oxide red walls, black ceiling, one blade of cold white. The whole cosmology in one frame.

**H-5 · The maker's mark**
> Macro close-up of a weathered hand scratching a tiny personal mark into the hidden underside of a finished bronze vessel, just before it is wrapped for transport. Workshop out of focus behind. Single warm practical light. 100mm macro, very shallow focus. Palette: warm bronze, soot-black skin, dark workshop. Intimate and quietly defiant.

**H-6 · The send-up**
> A crowd gathered at the base of an enormous ziggurat at dawn, watching a procession of porters carry finished goods up the vast stair — cloth, bronze, grain — toward the dark vault far above. The crowd is not celebrating. They are watching. Wide shot, cool dawn light, low contrast, mist. Palette: dusty rose dawn, red stone, muted indigo crowd.

### CHARACTERS

**C1 · The Ascendant**
> Portrait of a Bronze Age craftsman in their late twenties, indigo-dyed wool, burn scars on the forearms, soot in the creases of the hands, holding a single finished bronze object. Standing in a doorway between warm firelight behind and cold white light ahead. Half the face warm, half cold. 85mm portrait, shallow focus. Palette: fire orange and cold white split down the centre of the face. Determined, not heroic.

**C2 · The Assessor**
> Portrait of an aged official seated in a black stone tier, grey robes, clay tablet in lap, lit only from directly above so the eyes are in deep shadow. Utterly neutral expression. Not cruel — tired. 85mm, hard toplight, black background. Palette: cold white, grey, basalt black.

**C3 · The Broker**
> Three-quarter portrait of a handsome, well-fed man in fine cream wool with a heavy cylinder seal on a cord at his throat, mid-laugh, warm and completely convincing. Warm key light, dark basalt behind. 50mm, shallow focus. Palette: cream, gold, warm skin against black. The most trustworthy face in the world bible.

**C4 · The Returned**
> A gaunt figure in ruined, once-fine white Heaven linen, now filthy, sitting on the red rock floor of Hell with a crowd keeping its distance. Hard noon sun, harsh shadows. Wide-ish 35mm, subject small, isolated by empty ground. Palette: dirty white against oxide red. Unmistakably telling the truth and unmistakably not believed.

**C5 · The Gatekeeper**
> A Firmament guard in black basalt-grey, standing at the narrow gate, lit from above. On the wall beside him, barely visible, an old scratched maker's mark — the same style as a Hell craftsman's. His own. Medium shot, 50mm, hard toplight. Palette: basalt black, cold white. The detail is the story; don't light it specially.

### THE ARC — same room, four eras *(to prove the franchise)*

**A1 · Rome, 64 CE**
> The same narrow vertical court, now in marble and porphyry. Tiers of senators and patrons above, a petitioner on a round mosaic disc below, hard light from an oculus far overhead. Anamorphic wide, low angle from behind the petitioner. Palette: marble white, porphyry red, black shadow, one spot of imperial purple.

**A2 · The chartered company, 1680**
> The same narrow vertical court, now dark oak panelling and brass. Tiers of periwigged company directors above with ledgers, a weathered ship's master on a worn circle of floorboard below, hard light from a high skylight. Candle smoke haze. Palette: oak brown, brass, candle gold, black.

**A3 · The pitch room, 2008**
> The same narrow vertical court, now black glass and steel. Tiers of investors above behind a wall of screens, a single founder standing on a circular rug below, hard overhead track lighting, haze. Anamorphic wide, low angle from behind the founder. Palette: black glass, cold white LED, one red recording light.

**A4 · The model, near future**
> The same narrow vertical court, now almost featureless — soft white volume, no visible seats, forty-two small lenses recessed in the walls above, a single person on a circular floor plate below, lit from everywhere and nowhere. No shadows at all. Palette: white on white, one faint amber indicator. The absence of faces is the horror.

## 8. Open questions for the next pass

1. **Mesopotamia or Egypt** for Game I. I've argued Mesopotamia; the Hall of
   Two Truths is the counter-case.
2. **Does the player ever reach Heaven in Game I?** My instinct: yes, and it is
   the worst thing that happens to them.
3. **Is the Broker's percentage literal?** Whether it's a cut of *time* — of
   your remaining hourglass — or something less supernatural changes the genre.
4. **How much of the turn do we show in Game I?** Probably: one frame, once,
   and never mention it.
